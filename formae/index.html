Letters rise from quiet strokes, where curves meet stems and every counter holds a breath of light. A typeface begins as rhythm: narrow forms gather speed, broad forms create pause, and careful spacing turns separate signs into a fluent voice. Serifs guide the eye across a line, while round bowls and open apertures give each word its color. From the smallest caption to the loudest title, letters balance clarity with character. They carry memory, mood, and meaning through books, screens, streets, and rooms. An alphabet is a system, yet each glyph keeps its own gesture, proportion, tension, and grace.

Printing began as a craft of patience, each page proofed by hand before a single sheet left the press. Compositors set every line by touch, learning a typeface rhythm through repetition. Ink settled unevenly into paper fibers, giving early books a texture modern screens still try to imitate. That same attentiveness now guides digital type: proofed at small sizes, tested against real paragraphs, corrected until the page reads as one calm voice.

A letter starts with a mark and grows through measured choices. The curve of an o teaches the rhythm of every round form; the stem of an n sets the pace of a word. Designers adjust weight, contrast, width, and spacing until letters feel related without becoming identical. Tiny changes around a shoulder or terminal can alter the tone of an entire paragraph. In print, ink softens the edges; on a screen, pixels ask for clarity. A useful typeface must speak quietly in long text and confidently in display sizes. Its alphabet becomes a family of shapes, joined by proportion, detail, rhythm, and shared intent. It lives.

Regular / 32

Renaissance printers cut each letter by hand and corrected it by eye, adjusting stroke and counter until a whole page of type read as one even, continuous colour of ink rather than a row of separate signs.

Regular / 24

Long before phototypesetting or digital type, the printed page still carried the memory of the metal it came from: a slight unevenness in the impression, a rhythm set by hand rather than by a machine, and a texture that later, smoother technologies would spend a century trying to either erase or imitate.

Regular / 16

Formae keeps a trace of that history in its own structure: cut counters, calm proportions and a measured contrast between thick and thin strokes, paired with oldstyle figures, a dedicated stylistic set, discretionary ligatures and a matching Old English companion face. The result is a working display serif for maps, posters, packaging, book covers and any headline that has to hold an entire page together at a glance, carrying a printed, historical register without ever slipping into pastiche or costume drama.
